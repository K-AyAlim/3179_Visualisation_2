Fur Real: Who Are Australia's Cats?

Exploring the ups and downs of domestic cat breeds in Australia

Purebred cats are beautiful, but the reality is that most cats in Australia aren’t purebred. What truly shapes their lives isn’t their breed, but how we care for them. So let’s look past the pedigree and into the realities of cat life in Australia, from the homes they share with us to the shelters some end up in, and how we can keep them safe.

How popular are cats in Australia?

1 in 3 homes has a cat!

The 2025 Pet Ownership Study revealed that cats are the second most popular pet in Australia, so much so that 33% of all households in the country have a purring pet.

Most cats aren’t purebred, but they are all claw-some! In this section we will breakdown the breed frequencies in the places that publish their data and explore that, while purebreds get a lot of attention, they play a much smaller part than you think.

People have favourites

Among pedigreed cats, preferences differ noticeably between regions. Ragdolls account for 37% of Townsville’s purebred cats, Burmese for 32% of those in Surf Coast, and British Shorthairs for 17% of those in Golden Plains.

But out of 100 cats, more than 4 in 5 have no pedigree!

Purebred cats tend to receive the most attention, yet they make up only 13% of the registered cats in these three councils. The remaining 87% are mixed (76%), crossbred (6%) or of unknown breed (5%). A lack of pedigree does not make a cat any less deserving of care, and every one of them is owed the same responsibility from its owner.

Let's explore the essentials of cat care and what every owner must do to protect their pet and why they should do it. Starting with MICROCHIPPING! Which is the act of implaning a miniscule chip just under the cat's skin that holds crucial information (e.g. owner details) and location data.

What happens when they get lost?

Thankfully, most cats that enter the Royal Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (RSPCA) shelters are either rehomed or reunited with their owners. However, a cat can only be returned if it can be identified, which is why the RSPCA states that microchips are the best way for a lost pet to be returned home.

But older cats wait longer...

Kittens are adopted quickly, whereas adolescent and adult cats are almost twice as likely to wait more than 60 days for a new home (20% compared with 11%). This delay carries real consequences. In many states, pounds are only required to hold a lost cat for 4 days, or 8 days if it is microchipped. Although most shelters strive to be no-kill, every additional cat adds pressure, and for an unidentified cat a single day can be the difference between life and death.

...but some never make it back

In 2023–24, the RSPCA euthanised 5,148 cats. For this organisation medical reasons accounted for 41% of these cases and behavioural reasons for a further 35%.

Many of these outcomes could have been prevented had the cats been microchipped, registered and desexed, as desexed cats are less likely to roam, fight or breed, and identified cats can be returned home before their condition worsens. Not to mention the RSPCA is only one shelter provider, in general this number is estimated to be around 50,000 cats euthanised across all shelters in the country.

4Why it Matters: Desexing!

Besides microchipping, another esssential part of cat care that we briefly touched on was DESEXING! Which is the act of spaying or neutering a cat in order to surgically steralise them, preventing unwanted litters and certain health issues.

Many Unknown Breed Cats do not get desexed

Cats with no recorded breed are far less likely to be desexed. This might potentially be due to the cost of desexing, and how some people dont want to or cant spend that on a cat without a pedigree

Desexing on a Budget

Desexing a cat costs an average of between $185 in Tasmania and $245 in New South Wales. That is a one-off cost, far less than raising an unplanned litter of kittens, and many councils and the National Desexing Network offer discounted desexing for owners who need help.

But desexing must occur because the consequences are severe!

Even within one city, some suburbs have far more cats, and far fewer desexed, than others.

Feral cats killing native species

Feral cats kill an estimated 2 billion reptiles, birds, frogs and mammals across Australia every year, most of them native species. Many feral cats are descended from pets that were abandoned or allowed to breed, which is why desexing is one of the most effective ways to stop the feral population from growing.

5Why it Matters: Keep cats indoors! ฅ(•˕ •マ⟆

Keeping cats indoors keeps everyone safe: you, your pet and Australia's native critters

Ecosystem Annihilators

On average, each roaming pet cat kills 110 native animals a year: 40 reptiles, 38 birds and 32 mammals. Of Australia’s 3.8 million pet cats, 2.7 million (71%) are allowed to roam, and together they kill an estimated 241 million native animals every year, with devastating effects on local ecosystems. Keeping cats contained protects wildlife as much as it protects the cats themselves.

Don't worry, you’re not alone

Containing a cat is a widely supported choice. In a 2024 survey of South Australians, 72% supported keeping cats contained, while only 10% opposed it.

Their safety is in YOUR hands!

Keep your cat safe by registering, desexing and microchipping them!
